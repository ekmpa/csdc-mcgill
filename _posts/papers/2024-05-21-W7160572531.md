abstract_fr: 'Intergroup bias has long been understood as an individual trait. This
  individual-trait perspective is inconsistent with emerging theories that conceptualise
  implicit bias as influenced by features of one’s greater context. Yet “context”
  is a broad term with many possible interpretations, and theoretical models of bias
  are not yet refined enough to specify which aspects of context matter, and when.
  Toward this goal, the current research observes variations in bias across one theoretically
  important operationalization of context: geographic location. In a quasi-experimental
  longitudinal design, we tracked participants’ (n = 522) intergroup biases for a
  year as they moved across geographies, observing the effect of geography on implicit
  and explicit biases towards fictitious, racial, and cultural-linguistic groups.
  There were a number of patterns that would have been consistent with the broad idea
  of geography causing variations in bias, but 19 of the 24 analyses were not consistent
  with this idea. The remaining five analyses offered small, tentative evidence suggesting
  specific local factors might be associated with changes in bias. Together, results
  provide important constraints on theories of how context might shape bias, enabling
  refinement of theoretical models.'
