abstract_fr: Cities have long represented and promoted the power of political 
  and religious elites, typically by reproducing symbolic or religious orders in
  material form. This chapter reviews the conceptual and historical 
  relationships among cities, states and national identities. Industrialization 
  gave states vastly increased power to reshape urban landscapes and provided an
  incentive to build national identity through the symbolic and physical 
  (re)construction of cities and monuments. Colonial powers used - and often 
  tested - similar strategies to assert their authority in overseas possessions.
  Techniques, styles and standards of urban planning were codified and promoted 
  through international networks of professional planners. In Europe and North 
  America, state-driven urban planning and 'monumental' national symbols fell 
  from favour in the latter half of the twentieth century. In contrast, 
  postcolonial states in the Global South embraced these traditions, and 'new 
  cities' are now commonly part of their strategies for nation building, 
  economic development and even geopolitical power.
