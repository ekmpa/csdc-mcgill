abstract_fr: "It has been suggested that exercise may facilitate smoking cessation
  and should be initiated prior to a scheduled quit date. PURPOSE: The purpose of
  this study was two-fold: (1) to determine smoking history, demographic variables,
  and fitness parameters associated with progress toward quit attempt, and (2) to
  examine the effects of home- versus facility-based exercise intervention, as well
  as exercise adherence counseling versus exercise prescription only, on progress
  to quit date in a sample of urban women smokers. METHODS: Women cigarette smokers
  (N = 221) aged 18 to 55 (M = 40.5 ± 10.2 yrs) provided information on cigarettes
  smoked per day (CPD, M = 16.8 ± 7.2), completed treadmill exercise fitness testing
  (V02Peak, M = 24.3 ± 7.2 ml/kg/min), and were assigned to either a home-based (HB)
  or facility-based (FB) exercise program inclusive of exercise prescription (Rx)
  only or Rx plus exercise adherence counseling (Rx+) 3 weeks prior to their planned
  quit date. RESULTS: By quit date, 15.8% (n = 35) had abandoned their quit attempt.
  Fisher's exact corner cells tests indicated that HB and FB groups had similar drop-out
  rates, 12% and 19.5%, respectively (Chi-Square (N = 221, df = 1) = 2.29, p = .14).
  Similarly, quit date drop-out rate was similar for Rx+ (14.2 %) and Rx (17.4%) groups
  (Chi-Square (N = 221, df = 1) = .44, p = .58). Women abandoning their smoking cessation
  quit attempt were not significantly different with respect to age, race, income,
  BMI, fitness, or smoking frequency (CPD) from women who progressed to their quit
  date. CONCLUSIONS: These data suggest that a small but significant number of women
  abandon their smoking cessation attempt prior to their scheduled quit date. This
  early abandonment of quit attempt does not appear to be related to demographic factors,
  smoking frequency, or fitness. It appears that home- and facility-based exercise
  is equally effective in helping women progress to their quit attempt. Further research
  may examine the effects of psychosocial variables (i.e. exercise barriers, self-efficacy,
  depression) on progress to smoking cessation. Support contributed by: NIDA12503"
