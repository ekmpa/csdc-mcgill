abstract_fr: It is well known that people’s concepts of traits such as competence
  and dominance vary (Stolier et al., 2020). For example, one might think of competence
  differently depending on whether they are asked if a young woman versus an old man
  is competent. It seems reasonable to predict that people’s trait concepts also vary
  depending on who the target is. For example, research on impression formation indicates
  that a target’s facial features have an impact on what impressions are made about
  them (Hummert et al., 1997; Rodríguez-Gómez et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2019). Knowing
  that traits vary, and that they vary depending on who the target is, we can apply
  this understanding to specific traits, such as competence, dominance, and trustworthiness.
  How does one’s idea of competence, dominance, or trustworthiness vary depending
  on who they are evaluating? If asked, many individuals would likely describe a competent
  older man as being experienced and intelligent, and a competent younger woman as
  being attentive, and having good interpersonal skills. We can thus see that the
  same trait, competence, is conceptualized very differently depending on who it is
  being applied to. We can accept that ideas around competence vary depending on who
  the target is, but we are left asking why this happens. Why do trait concepts vary
  depending on who is being judged? One potential mediator to explain differences
  in trait concepts is assumed context.
