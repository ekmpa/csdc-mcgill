abstract_fr: In this article, we propose that wealthy donors give foreign aid to developing
  countries to&#13;\nfacilitate political adjustment, such as compensation for losers
  and side payments to influential&#13;\nelite constituencies, towards mutually profitable
  economic reform. Only democratic developing&#13;\ncountries can credibly commit
  to using fungible revenue in ways that benefit the donor,&#13;\nso the adjustment
  effect only applies to democracies. A quantitative test against data on preferential&#13;\ntrading
  agreements lends strong support to the theory. Strikingly, fully democratic&#13;\ndeveloping
  countries that form a preferential trading agreement obtain a threefold increase
  in&#13;\nforeign aid in the short run. Additional tests show that this increase
  is not driven by macroeconomic&#13;\ndifficulties and that the beneficial effect
  on foreign aid is temporary. Both findings&#13;\nare consistent with the theory.
  An important implication of these results is that if foreign aid&#13;\nfacilitates
  economic reform through preferential trading agreements, previous research could&#13;\nhave
  underestimated the benefits thereof.
